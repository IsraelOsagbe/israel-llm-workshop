1.1 Conservation Laws in Differential & Integral Form

A conservation law states that the rate of change of a conserved quantity U in a control volume V equals the net flux through its boundary ∂V plus sources:

∂/∂t ∫_V U dV + ∮_∂V F(U)·n dS = ∫_V S(U) dV

Applying the divergence theorem yields the differential form (valid where U is smooth):

∂U/∂t + ∇·F(U) = S(U)

For fluid dynamics, the conserved vector and fluxes in 3D are:

U = [ ρ, ρu, ρv, ρw, ρE ]^T F^x = [ ρu, ρu²+p, ρuv, ρuw, u(ρE+p) ]^T F^y = [ ρv, ρuv, ρv²+p, ρvw, v(ρE+p) ]^T F^z = [ ρw, ρuw, ρvw, ρw²+p, w(ρE+p) ]^T

with equation of state p = (γ−1)(ρE − ½ρ(u²+v²+w²)). The system is hyperbolic — the Jacobian ∂F/∂U has real eigenvalues and a complete set of eigenvectors.

1.2 Quasi-Linear Form & Characteristic Structure

In 1D, the system becomes quasi-linear:

∂U/∂t + A(U) ∂U/∂x = 0, A(U) = ∂F/∂U

The eigenvalues of A are the wave speeds: λ₁ = u−c, λ₂ = u, λ₃ = u, λ₄ = u, λ₅ = u+c (1D Euler). The right eigenvectors define the characteristic fields — genuinely nonlinear (acoustic) or linearly degenerate (entropy, vorticity).

Characteristic Decomposition

Any perturbation can be decomposed into characteristic variables W = R⁻¹U where R is the matrix of right eigenvectors. The system diagonalizes:

∂W/∂t + Λ ∂W/∂x = 0

This is the theoretical basis for Riemann solvers and characteristic boundary conditions.

Entropy Condition

For a convex entropy function η(U) with entropy flux ψ(U) satisfying ∂ψ/∂U = ∂η/∂U · ∂F/∂U, the second law requires:

∂η/∂t + ∇·ψ ≤ 0

Across a shock, the Lax entropy condition selects the physically admissible solution: characteristics must enter the shock (for genuinely nonlinear fields). This is equivalent to the Liu entropy condition for systems.

1.3 Well-Posedness of Initial-Boundary Value Problems

A problem is well-posed (Hadamard) if: (1) a solution exists, (2) it is unique, (3) it depends continuously on data.

Hyperbolic IBVP Theory (Kreiss, Gustafsson–Kreiss–Oliger)

For a boundary at x = 0 with outward normal n = (−1, 0, 0), the number of boundary conditions required equals the number of incoming characteristics (eigenvalues with λ·n > 0). For subsonic inflow (u > 0, u < c): 4 incoming (λ₁, λ₂, λ₃, λ₄) → 4 BCs. Subsonic outflow: 1 incoming (λ₁) → 1 BC. Supersonic inflow: 5 incoming → 5 BCs. Supersonic outflow: 0 incoming → 0 BCs.

Boundary conditions must be imposed on the incoming characteristic variables, not primitive variables. For example, at a subsonic inflow:

W₁ = W₁^∞, W₂ = W₂^∞, W₃ = W₃^∞, W₄ = W₄^∞ (specified) W₅ computed from interior (extrapolated)

Imposing primitive variables (ρ, u, p) directly at boundaries generates spurious reflections — a common error in student codes.

1.4 Viscous Terms & Parabolic Regularization

The full Navier–Stokes system adds viscous fluxes F_v(U, ∇U):

∂U/∂t + ∇·F(U) = ∇·F_v(U, ∇U)

This makes the system mixed hyperbolic–parabolic. The viscous terms introduce second derivatives, requiring boundary conditions on velocity and temperature (no-slip, adiabatic/isothermal wall). The mathematical type changes at boundaries: the inviscid problem needs fewer BCs than the viscous problem — the boundary layer resolves this discrepancy.

Boundary Layer Scaling

At high Re, introduce stretched coordinate η = y/δ with δ ~ Re⁻¹/². The leading-order equations are the Prandtl boundary layer equations — parabolic in the streamwise direction. This singular perturbation structure is why high-Re flows are numerically stiff.

1.5 Incompressible Limit & Pressure-Poisson Equation

As Ma → 0, the compressible system becomes singular (acoustic wave speeds → ∞). The incompressible limit is a differential-algebraic system:

∇·u = 0 ∂u/∂t + u·∇u = −∇p/ρ + ν∇²u + g

Taking divergence of momentum and using continuity yields the pressure-Poisson equation:

∇²p = −ρ ∇·(u·∇u) + ∇·(ρg)

This is an elliptic constraint — pressure is determined globally and instantaneously. The incompressible system is well-posed with boundary conditions: u specified on all boundaries (Dirichlet) or ∂u/∂n specified (Neumann) with compatibility condition ∮ u·n dS = 0.

Helmholtz–Hodge Decomposition

Any vector field can be decomposed as u = u_div + ∇φ where ∇·u_div = 0. The projection operator P = I − ∇∇⁻²∇· extracts the divergence-free component. This is the continuous foundation of projection methods (Chorin, 1968).

Socratic Questions

Question 1: Prove that for the 1D Euler equations, the entropy function η = −ρs (where s = ln(p/ρ^γ)) is convex in the conserved variables U = (ρ, ρu, ρE). Why is convexity essential for the entropy inequality to select the correct weak solution?

Question 2: Consider a 1D subsonic outflow boundary (0 < u < c). How many boundary conditions are mathematically required? Derive the characteristic boundary condition for pressure in terms of the incoming Riemann invariant. What happens if you instead impose p = p_∞ directly?

Question 3: Show that the pressure-Poisson equation ∇²p = −ρ∇·(u·∇u) is obtained by taking the divergence of the momentum equation and enforcing ∇·u = 0. Why does this equation require a Neumann boundary condition for well-posedness? Derive the correct Neumann condition at a solid wall.

Question 4: The inviscid compressible Euler equations at a solid wall require only one boundary condition (flow tangency: u·n = 0). The viscous Navier–Stokes equations require three (no-slip: u = 0, plus temperature condition). Explain this discrepancy using the concept of singular perturbation and the boundary layer. How does this affect numerical boundary conditions for high-Re flows?
