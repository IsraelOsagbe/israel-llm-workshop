Conservation Laws: The Foundation

Fluid dynamics rests on three fundamental conservation principles, applied to a control volume (a fixed region in space) or a material volume (a moving blob of fluid):

  1. Mass conservation — Matter cannot be created or destroyed
  2. Momentum conservation — Newton's second law for a fluid element
  3. Energy conservation — First law of thermodynamics for a fluid element

Each gives a partial differential equation (PDE). Together they form a closed system describing the fluid state: velocity u = (u, v, w), pressure p, density ρ, temperature T, and internal energy e.

Mass Conservation (Continuity Equation)

For a control volume, the rate of mass increase inside equals the net mass flux through its boundary:

∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρu) = 0

In Cartesian coordinates:

∂ρ/∂t + ∂(ρu)/∂x + ∂(ρv)/∂y + ∂(ρw)/∂z = 0

Incompressible flow (constant density, e.g., water, low-speed air):

∇·u = 0  →  ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y + ∂w/∂z = 0

This is a kinematic constraint — the velocity field must be divergence-free. It couples the velocity components without involving pressure directly.

Momentum Conservation (Navier–Stokes Equations)

Newton's second law: rate of momentum change = sum of forces. Forces on a fluid element are:

  • Pressure forces — normal stress, −∇p
  • Viscous forces — shear stress, ∇·τ where τ is the viscous stress tensor
  • Body forces — gravity, electromagnetic, etc., ρg

For a Newtonian fluid (linear stress–strain rate relation):

ρ(∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = −∇p + μ∇²u + (μ/3 + λ)∇(∇·u) + ρg

where μ is dynamic viscosity and λ is bulk viscosity.

Incompressible, constant-μ Navier–Stokes:

ρ(∂u/∂t + u·∇u) = −∇p + μ∇²u + ρg
∇·u = 0

The term u·∇u is the convective acceleration — it makes the equations nonlinear and is the source of turbulence.

Energy Conservation

For compressible flow, temperature/density changes matter. The energy equation (first law) in terms of temperature:

ρc_p(∂T/∂t + u·∇T) = ∇·(k∇T) + Φ + Q̇

where c_p is specific heat, k is thermal conductivity, Φ is viscous dissipation, and Q̇ is heat sources.

For incompressible flow with constant properties, the energy equation decouples from momentum — you solve momentum first, then plug the velocity field into the energy equation. This is why many introductory CFD courses focus on isothermal incompressible flow.

Equation of State

To close the system, we need a relation between ρ, p, and T. For ideal gases:

p = ρRT

For liquids, density is often treated as constant (incompressible) or a weak function of pressure/temperature.

Compressible vs. Incompressible Flow: Channel Example

The distinction between compressible and incompressible flow fundamentally changes the governing equations, numerical methods, and physical phenomena. The key parameter is the Mach number Ma = U/c (flow speed / speed of sound). Generally, Ma < 0.3 → incompressible; Ma > 0.3 → compressible effects matter.

Channel Flow Example: Incompressible (Water)

Water at 20°C, ρ ≈ 998 kg/m³, μ ≈ 1.0×10⁻³ Pa·s, flowing at U = 2 m/s in a circular pipe of diameter D = 0.05 m.

  • Re = ρUD/μ ≈ 99,800 (turbulent)
  • Ma = U/c ≈ 2/1480 ≈ 0.0013 ≪ 0.3 → incompressible
  • Pressure drop from Darcy–Weisbach: Δp = f(L/D)(ρU²/2)
  • Density, temperature essentially constant along pipe
  • Velocity profile fully developed, unchanged downstream

Channel Flow Example: Compressible (Air)

Air at 300 K, 100 kPa, ρ ≈ 1.16 kg/m³, μ ≈ 1.85×10⁻⁵ Pa·s, c ≈ 347 m/s, flowing at U = 300 m/s in same pipe.

  • Re = ρUD/μ ≈ 940,000 (turbulent)
  • Ma = U/c ≈ 0.86 → compressible, transonic
  • Fanno flow analysis: friction causes Mach number to approach 1 (choking)
  • Density decreases significantly along pipe (ρ ∝ 1/√(1 + fL/D))
  • Velocity increases along pipe to maintain mass flow ρU = constant
  • Temperature drops due to adiabatic expansion
  • If pipe is long enough, flow chokes at Ma = 1 — maximum mass flow reached

Boundary Conditions: The Other Half of the Problem

The PDEs need boundary conditions (BCs) to have a unique solution. Common types:

TypePhysical MeaningTypical Use
Dirichlet (fixed value)u = u_wall, T = T_wallNo-slip wall, fixed temperature
Neumann (fixed gradient)∂u/∂n = 0, ∂T/∂n = q/kSymmetry plane, prescribed heat flux
Robin (mixed)αu + β∂u/∂n = γConvective heat transfer, slip walls
Periodicu(x) = u(x+L)Repeating geometries, DNS of homogeneous turbulence

Inlet/Outlet BCs deserve special care — poorly posed outlet conditions reflect waves back into the domain and corrupt the solution.

Check Your Understanding

Question 1: The incompressible continuity equation ∇·u = 0 contains no pressure. How does pressure get determined in an incompressible flow simulation? (Hint: think about what the momentum equation provides.)

Question 2: The term u·∇u is called "convective acceleration." Write it out in 2D Cartesian coordinates. Why does this term make the Navier–Stokes equations nonlinear? What would the equations look like without it?

Question 3: At a solid wall, the no-slip condition says u = 0. But the Navier–Stokes equations are second-order in space — they need two boundary conditions per velocity component. What is the second condition at a wall, and where does it come from physically?
