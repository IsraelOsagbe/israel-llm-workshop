From PDEs to Linear Systems

After discretization (Lesson 3), the continuous PDEs become a large system of nonlinear algebraic equations. For the steady incompressible Navier–Stokes on a mesh with N cells:

  • Unknowns: p (N), u, v, w (3N) → 4N total
  • Equations: Continuity (N) + Momentum x3 (3N) → 4N equations

This is a sparse, nonlinear, coupled system. We solve it iteratively:

  1. Linearize the equations (Newton, Picard, or segregated approach)
  2. Solve the resulting linear system
  3. Update the solution
  4. Repeat until converged

Segregated vs. Coupled Solution

Segregated (Pressure-Based) Solvers — Most Common

Solve momentum and continuity sequentially, iterating between them:

  1. Guess pressure field p*
  2. Solve momentum equations for velocity u* using p*
  3. Velocity u* won't satisfy continuity. Derive a pressure correction equation from continuity.
  4. Solve for pressure correction p', correct pressure and velocity
  5. Repeat until mass and momentum residuals are small

Famous algorithms: SIMPLE (Semi-Implicit Method for Pressure-Linked Equations), SIMPLEC, PISO. OpenFOAM uses PISO for transient, SIMPLE for steady.

Coupled (Density-Based) Solvers

Solve all equations simultaneously as one big system. Uses the compressible form with an equation of state. Natural for high-speed compressible flow (shocks, supersonics). Less robust at low Mach numbers due to stiffness.

Linear Solvers: The Inner Loop

Each segregated iteration requires solving linear systems like Ax = b where A is a large sparse matrix (millions of rows). Direct solvers (LU decomposition) are too expensive — we use iterative linear solvers.

Stationary Iterative Methods

  • Gauss–Seidel / SOR: Update each unknown using latest neighbor values. Simple, slow convergence.
  • Jacobi: Use only old values. Parallelizable but slower.

Krylov Subspace Methods (The Standard)

  • CG (Conjugate Gradient): For symmetric positive-definite matrices
  • GMRES (Generalized Minimal Residual): For general non-symmetric matrices
  • BiCGSTAB: Bi-conjugate gradient stabilized, less memory than GMRES

These converge in at most N iterations (exact in exact arithmetic), but we stop early when residual is small enough.

Preconditioning — The Key to Fast Convergence

Krylov methods converge slowly for ill-conditioned matrices. A preconditioner M ≈ A transforms the system:

M⁻¹Ax = M⁻¹b

so the eigenvalues cluster near 1. Common preconditioners:

  • ILU(k): Incomplete LU factorization (drop small fill-ins)
  • Algebraic Multigrid (AMG): Solve on a hierarchy of coarser grids — the gold standard for CFD
  • Block Jacobi / Additive Schwarz: Domain decomposition for parallelism

Multigrid: Why It Works

Standard iterative smoothers (Gauss–Seidel) quickly eliminate high-frequency errors (oscillatory on the grid) but struggle with low-frequency errors (smooth, long-wavelength).

Multigrid fixes this by:

  1. Smoothing on the fine grid (kills high-frequency error)
  2. Restricting the residual to a coarser grid (low-frequency error becomes high-frequency there)
  3. Solving (or smoothing) on the coarse grid
  4. Prolongating the correction back to the fine grid
  5. Repeat recursively (V-cycle, W-cycle, F-cycle)

This gives mesh-independent convergence — the number of iterations to reach a given tolerance stays constant as you refine the mesh. For CFD, geometric multigrid (structured) or algebraic multigrid (unstructured) is essential.

Turbulence Modeling: The Closure Problem

The Navier–Stokes equations are exact. But for turbulent flow, the smallest scales (Kolmogorov scale) are far too small to resolve on any practical mesh for engineering Reynolds numbers. We must model the effect of unresolved scales.

RANS (Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes)

Decompose velocity: u = ū + u' (mean + fluctuation). Average the equations:

ρ(∂ū/∂t + ū·∇ū) = −∇p̄ + μ∇²ū − ∇·(ρ u'u') + ρg

The Reynolds stress tensor −ρ u'u' is unknown — the closure problem.

Common models:

  • Eddy viscosity models: −ρ u'u' = μ_t(∇ū + ∇ū^T) − (2/3)ρkI
  • k–ε (two-equation): Transport equations for turbulent kinetic energy k and dissipation ε. Robust, standard for industrial RANS.
  • k–ω SST: Blends k–ω near walls with k–ε in free stream. Better for adverse pressure gradients, separation. Industry default for aerodynamics.
  • Reynolds Stress Models (RSM): Solve transport equations for each Reynolds stress component. More physics, more cost, less robust.

LES (Large Eddy Simulation)

Resolve large energy-containing eddies, model only the small subgrid scales. Much more accurate than RANS for unsteady flows, but 10–1000x more expensive. Requires fine mesh and time-accurate integration.

DNS (Direct Numerical Simulation)

Resolve all scales down to Kolmogorov. No modeling. Only for fundamental research at low Re (Re_τ ~ 1000 max on today's supercomputers).

Convergence: When Do You Stop?

Three criteria, all should be satisfied:

  1. Residuals drop: Normalized residuals (mass, momentum, energy, turbulence) fall 3–4 orders of magnitude
  2. Monitored quantities stabilize: Drag, lift, pressure drop, mass flow at outlets — stop changing
  3. Global balances satisfied: Mass in = mass out (to machine precision), energy in = energy out

Warning: Low residuals ≠ converged solution. A solution can have flat residuals but wrong physics (e.g., wrong turbulence model, bad boundary conditions). Always check monitored quantities.
