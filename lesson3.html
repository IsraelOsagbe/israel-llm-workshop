The Discretization Challenge

The Navier–Stokes equations are continuous PDEs. A computer can only store and manipulate a finite set of numbers. Discretization is the process of converting the continuous problem into a discrete algebraic system that a computer can solve.

Three main decisions must be made:

  1. Mesh (grid): How to divide the domain into discrete cells/points
  2. Discretization method: How to approximate derivatives (finite difference, finite volume, finite element, spectral...)
  3. Variable arrangement: Where to store unknowns (collocated vs. staggered)

Meshes: Structured vs. Unstructured

Structured Meshes

Points indexed by (i, j, k) in 3D. Neighbors are (i±1, j, k), etc. Advantages:

  • Simple data structures, fast access
  • Easy high-order schemes
  • Efficient memory access patterns

Disadvantage: Very hard to fit complex geometries (curved boundaries, internal features).

Unstructured Meshes

Arbitrary connectivity — each cell stores its neighbor list. Elements: triangles/tetrahedra, quadrilaterals/hexahedra, pyramids, prisms. Advantages:

  • Handles arbitrarily complex geometries
  • Local refinement easy (hanging nodes, polyhedral cells)

Disadvantage: More memory, indirect addressing, harder high-order accuracy.

Hybrid Meshes

Structured boundary-layer meshes (prisms/hexahedra) near walls for accuracy, unstructured tetrahedra/polyhedra in the far field. This is the industry standard for external aerodynamics.

Finite Difference Method (FDM)

Approximate derivatives directly using Taylor series. For a uniform 1D grid with spacing h:

First derivative (central difference, 2nd-order):

du/dx ≈ (u_{i+1} − u_{i−1}) / (2h)

Second derivative (central difference, 2nd-order):

d²u/dx² ≈ (u_{i+1} − 2u_i + u_{i−1}) / h²

First derivative (upwind, 1st-order):

du/dx ≈ (u_i − u_{i−1}) / h   (for positive flow direction)

FDM is simple but requires structured grids. It approximates the differential form of the PDE.

Finite Volume Method (FVM) — The CFD Workhorse

FVM integrates the PDE over each control volume (cell). The divergence theorem converts volume integrals of divergences to surface fluxes:

∫_V ∇·F dV = ∮_S F·n dS

For the continuity equation ∇·(ρu) = 0 integrated over cell P:

∑_faces (ρu·n)_f A_f = 0

where the sum is over all faces of the cell, A_f is face area, and n is the outward normal.

Key advantages of FVM:

  • Naturally conservative — fluxes leaving one cell enter its neighbor exactly
  • Works on any mesh (structured or unstructured)
  • Handles discontinuities (shocks) well
  • Matches the physical control-volume intuition

Almost all commercial CFD codes (ANSYS Fluent, OpenFOAM, STAR-CCM+) use FVM.

Finite Element Method (FEM)

Multiply the PDE by a test function, integrate over the domain, integrate by parts to move derivatives to the test function. Approximate the solution as a sum of basis functions (typically polynomials) defined on elements.

Strengths: Rigorous mathematical framework, natural high-order accuracy (p-refinement), excellent for structural mechanics and coupled problems.

In CFD: Used in COMSOL, some research codes, and increasingly for high-order LES/DNS. Less common in industrial RANS.

Variable Arrangement: Collocated vs. Staggered

Staggered Grid (Harlow–Welch, MAC method)

Pressure at cell centers, velocity components at face centers.

  • Pro: No pressure-velocity decoupling (checkerboard modes)
  • Con: Complex on unstructured meshes, multiple velocity storage locations

Collocated Grid

All variables (p, u, v, w) stored at cell centers.

  • Pro: Simple data structure, natural for unstructured FVM
  • Con: Checkerboard pressure oscillations if not careful
  • Fix: Rhie–Chow interpolation — interpolate face velocities using pressure gradients to couple p and u

Modern unstructured FVM codes almost universally use collocated grids with Rhie–Chow.

Order of Accuracy

A scheme is p-th order accurate if the truncation error scales as O(h^p).

  • 1st-order (upwind): Very diffusive, smears sharp gradients. Robust but inaccurate.
  • 2nd-order (central, linear upwind): Standard for production RANS. Good balance.
  • Higher-order (≥3rd): Less diffusion, better for LES/DNS. More complex, can oscillate near discontinuities.

Rule of thumb: For a given mesh, 2nd-order FVM typically gives engineering accuracy for RANS. For LES, you want at least 2nd-order, preferably higher.

Check Your Understanding

Question 1: Why does the finite volume method guarantee conservation? Write the discrete continuity equation for a cell and show that the flux leaving one cell equals the flux entering its neighbor.

Question 2: On a collocated grid, a "checkerboard" pressure field (alternating high/low in a checker pattern) produces zero pressure gradient at cell centers when using standard central differences. Explain why this happens, and how Rhie–Chow interpolation fixes it.

Question 3: You're simulating flow over a car with detailed underbody geometry (exhaust, suspension, diffuser). Would you choose a structured, unstructured, or hybrid mesh? Justify your choice with at least three reasons.
